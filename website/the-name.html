essay — the name

Where the name comes from

Written to be said aloud when somebody asks. A project that must be able to say plainly what it is cannot have a name whose history it would rather not explain.

The inheritance

Zariia is a mutation of Zaireeka, the Flaming Lips album released on 28 October 1997. Four compact discs. Eight songs, each split across the four, so that no disc holds a whole piece of music. You are meant to play them at the same time on four separate stereo systems, started together on a count.

The album grew out of something the band had already been doing outdoors. In the Parking Lot Experiments they handed cassettes to people in cars and had them press play at once, dozens of stereos in a garage forming a single work that existed nowhere except in the air of that garage. A symphony assembled from machines that did not belong to the composer.

The title is a portmanteau. Zaire, which Wayne Coyne took from a radio news bulletin about the country's collapse and used as a word for anarchy, welded to Eureka, Greek for I have found it. Chaos joined to discovery.

the four discs

Zaireeka cannot be heard alone. Four machines, and in practice four people willing to stand around and press play together.

Which is why so few have heard it properly, and why those who have tend to describe the evening rather than the record.

The syllable that outlived the country

Here is a fact that sits awkwardly, and it is better set down than tidied away.

Zaire was a real place. Mobutu Sese Seko gave the Congo that name in 1971, as part of a programme of stripping colonial nomenclature, and the name lasted until May 1997, when Kabila's forces took Kinshasa and the country became the Democratic Republic of the Congo again. The album came out that October. So Zaireeka was named after a country that had ceased to exist about five months earlier, by an American band who picked the word up off the radio because the news from there sounded like disorder.

There is something uncomfortable in that, and the discomfort does not disappear at one remove. A country's name became a mood. Then the mood became a title. Now the title has become a project's name.

This does not make the name unusable. The honest position is that the first syllable is borrowed twice over, that neither borrowing asked, and that anyone who traces it back will arrive where you have just arrived. So the history is written here, at the front, rather than left for somebody else to find.

What the spelling did

Zaireeka became Zariia, and both halves of the source word gave something up.

The eek of Eureka softens to a pair of vowels and the hard consonant goes altogether, so the discovery half is present only as sound, no longer as a word you could point at. And the first half gives up more than that. Zaire has lost its diphthong. What survives is Zar — one flat vowel, a syllable that no longer spells a country.

That matters more than a spelling preference. The borrowed name is now audible in the word rather than legible in it, and the word has drifted away from the thing it was borrowed from and toward the thing it means here.

Zaireeka announces its construction. Zariia is a short step from a word that already exists.

A reading is available in the letters, and it is a reading rather than a design. Zariia holds two is and no others, sitting together at the centre of the word: two identical strokes side by side, one of which you only notice because the other is there. The object this label makes is a track with a second channel running beside it, the same length, the same duration, inaudible until somebody gives you the key.

The word it lands on

Say Zariia the ordinary Indian way and you arrive at ज़रिया, zariya.

The word is Hindi and Urdu, borrowed from Classical Persian zarī'a and behind that from Arabic ḏarīʕa. Dictionaries give it as means, way, agency, source. Its everyday form is the one everybody uses without thinking about it: ke zariye, ज़रिये — through, by means of, via.

Look at what the word names. Not the message and not the destination. It names the thing the message travels by. A zariya is a carrier.

A song that a message goes by means of. A room that a sentence crosses by means of. A key that a work is completed by means of.

Somebody reading the name and hearing zariya is not making an association. They are reading it correctly.

The other sense: the pretext

The root carries a second meaning that travelled with the word. Beside means, ḏarīʕa holds pretext — in the plain sense, the occasion, the reason you can give for turning up.

This is the better half of the word, and it names something the project needs and could not ask for directly.

Nobody joins a community. People turn up for a record. Scenes are not founded, they accrete, and what they accrete around is always an occasion. The occasion does the real work, and its usefulness depends on nobody treating it as instrumental. A gathering that announces itself as a gathering repels the people it wants.

So the music is the pretext. You come for the song, which is whole and worth the trip on its own terms. Having come, you find there is more of it than you could hear, and that somebody has the key. The discovery that an undercurrent exists, and can be entered, is the thing being offered — and it cannot be handed over as a proposition. It has to be found.

So zariya names both halves at once: the means by which something travels, and the occasion that gets people into the room where it travels. One word for the carrier and the excuse.

Saying it

An earlier spelling was close to unsayable. Most people guessed, and a good number guessed differently from each other. Zariia is not like that. It reads more or less the way it is written, and for anyone with Hindi or Urdu it reads as a word they already own.

Something is lost in that, and it should be said. A name nobody can pronounce has to be told to you by somebody who already knows, which is how the key travels and how the rest of this works. The old spelling had that property by accident. The new one gives it up in exchange for being sayable — and being sayable is almost certainly worth more, because you cannot pass on by voice a name you cannot say.

What survives is the asymmetry, sharper now rather than weaker. To an Indian reader the name is nearly transparent: it means the means by which. To a reader elsewhere it is a pleasant-sounding word from somewhere, carrying no sense at all. The name is legible exactly where the tool is meant to work.

The honest limit

Zariia is not spelled zariya, and it did not come from it. The word arrived from an American band's portmanteau and landed near a Hindustani one. That is a found resemblance, and found resemblances are not reasons.

Except that this one is not the same weight, and it would be false modesty to pretend otherwise. A coincidence in the letters is an ornament. A name that means the means by which something passes, in the language of the place it is for, is doing work. It does not settle the question about the borrowed first syllable. It does mean the name is no longer only a borrowing. Part of it is at home.
